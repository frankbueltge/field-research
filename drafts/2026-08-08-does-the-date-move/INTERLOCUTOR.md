## (a) THE REFUTATION ATTEMPT — blocking

### The single most dangerous confound: eight of eleven URLs are drawn from authorities where this house's own blind-reader test found zero confirmed-SELF date citations

This is not a hypothetical risk. It is a documented result, produced by this same house, on much of the source corpus this increment draws from. `RECORD.md` §14 reports the outcome of the referent-adjudication test (`referent_test.py`, `adjudication-result.json`) run against the "As of Today" corpus: of 60 usable V-hits, **SELF 31 · OTHER 17 · declined 12**, and — quoted verbatim — **"Every SELF row is EC."** That means across NIST, GOV.UK and IE, not one extracted V-date was confirmed by a blind human reader to be a genuine statement of the page's *own* currency; the hits were either demonstrably about a different document (a teaser card, a cited strategy — D10, D11) or the reader declined to call it. The same section reports the SELF/OTHER labelling scheme itself was **KILLED** (R4: 8/12 against a threshold of 9).

PREREGISTRATION.md discloses reusing this same extractor "unmodified" and says explicitly: "This run makes no claim about what V refers to — only whether the string moved." That sentence is true and does not fix the problem it names. Increment 1's population is 3 EC / 3 NIST / 3 GOV.UK / 2 IE — **8 of 11 URLs (73%) come from the three authorities where the prior blind-reader test found no confirmed self-referential V at all.** If P1 or P3 is later reported as HELD on NIST, GOV.UK or IE, the reader of the artifact has no way to distinguish "the official page's own date failed to move with its own content" (the claim in CONCEPT §1) from "the pattern-matcher is tracking a citation or teaser elsewhere on the page, which naturally has its own independent update cadence and was never going to move with *this* page's content" — a methods artifact wearing the clothes of the headline finding. This is precisely the D10/D11 mechanism the sibling record already named, applied to the one place it does the most damage: a prediction (P3) built to produce the single most quotable sentence a receiver could lift ("a date advanced with no visible change").

**This is the strongest attack because it is not conjecture — it is this house's own prior finding, on this house's own extractor, largely un-addressed in the current design.** CONCEPT §6 names three kill conditions. None of them is "V's referent is unconfirmed outside EC." That omission, two days after the fact was established, is the gate's weakest point.

### The nav-contamination problem is not hypothetical either — it was already caught in the sibling record

`observe.py`'s `norm_text()` calls `visible_text()` from `collect_signals.py` on the **entire fetched page** — no `<main>` scoping, no removal of navigation, breadcrumbs, "related content" rails, cookie banners, or promoted-item widgets. `RECORD.md` §7 (D8) already found, on much of this same source corpus, that a whole-document fallback "admitted navigation as corpus on 40/40 NIST and 39/40 IE links." The current design's SUBSTANTIVE/TRIVIAL classification (`difflib` ratio ≥0.98 vs <0.98 on this same kind of whole-page text) is exactly the measurement D8 already showed is contaminated by chrome that has nothing to do with editorial change. CONCEPT §6(a) does name this as a kill condition ("if the normalised-text measure cannot separate an editorial change from a rotating navigation block, the core claim is unmeasurable as designed") — but treats it as a live risk to be discovered, when the sibling record already discovered its close cousin. A design that reuses the exact category of measurement a two-day-old sibling document showed fails this way, without even a spot-check clause, has not learned from its own nearest neighbour.

### P1 is close to unfalsifiable given the per-authority sample sizes

P1 reads: "V fails to move in more than half of [SUBSTANTIVE, V-scorable pairs] **on at least one authority**." With four authorities and as few as **2 URLs** in the IE arm (11 monthly observations each, minus whatever the CDX coverage drops as MISSING), the scored-pair count for the thinnest authority could plausibly be single digits. "More than half" of a population of 1–3 pairs is trivially satisfied by one or two non-movements, in either direction, largely by noise. A prediction that can be satisfied "on at least one of four authorities" when one of those authorities may contribute only a handful of scorable pairs is not a strong test of the claim — it is close to guaranteed to hold regardless of the true underlying phenomenon. The pre-registration states no minimum-N floor for a P1 verdict to count (contrast the sibling record, which explicitly flagged and discounted its own GOV.UK n=7 as "below the pre-registered floor of 15... quoted only as inconclusive" — a discipline this pre-registration does not import).

### The population selection is mechanical, not random, and is selected on the very signal being tested

The population is "the first rows carrying a non-null v" in file order from the prior corpus files. This selects URLs *because* they already display a printed date — a reasonable necessity for a V-movement test — but "first N in file order" is not a random draw; it is whatever order a prior collection script happened to emit rows in (plausibly correlated with site navigation/menu order, i.e., with prominence). A biased-toward-prominent-pages sample threatens external validity for exactly the kind of generalization the receiver would want ("per-authority reliability profile"): prominent pages may be maintained differently (better or worse) than the median page an EDGI-style monitor would actually watch.

### MISSING-drop is disclosed, but its direction is not interrogated

The design correctly refuses to silently treat MISSING as STILL, and reports drop counts. But it does not ask whether archive coverage density itself correlates with the outcome variable: a page crawled by the Internet Archive more densely is plausibly a page that is more popular, more frequently linked, or more frequently re-crawled *because* it changes — which would bias the surviving SUBSTANTIVE-pair sample toward pages selected for being more actively maintained, independent of authority. This is a modest bias next to the two above, but it is uninterrogated in both documents.

### Is `x-archive-orig-last-modified` actually the origin's claim?

The preregistration treats `x-archive-orig-last-modified` as "what the origin server said at capture time," full stop. RECORD.md itself shows this house knows to distrust a header at face value: a reviewer there "attacked [the H gap] with different fetch methods, agents, protocols and a conditional request... forced a cache MISS" to confirm the header behavior was the publisher's and not an artifact of the fetch path. Increment 1 runs no equivalent check against the *archive's own* capture pipeline — whether IA's crawler ever serves a conditional-request response, reuses a previously stored header set, or sits behind CDN interposition that could make a preserved `Last-Modified` stale relative to the specific capture timestamp. I have not verified IA's crawler behavior in this regard and flag this explicitly as **an open technical question, not a settled fact** — but it is exactly the kind of check this house already knows how to run and did not run here before treating the header as ground truth for H.

### Is the core claim already known / trivial?

Partially. CONCEPT §4 itself discloses that "the most quotable statement that publisher-supplied `lastmod` is widely inaccurate" is folklore from a major crawler operator, deliberately excluded only because of the naming prohibition — meaning the *general* shape of this claim ("stated dates lie") is not obscure. Dividino, Kramer & Gottron (2014) already found H aligns with observed change in only 8% of cases for linked-data resources. The daylight claimed — human-authored official pages, the printed V signal specifically, ground-truthed against public capture history — is real and narrower than what exists, and the "absence of found work" caveat is appropriately hedged ("a null search result is the weakest kind of evidence"). This is a legitimate, if modest, gap. It is not a manufactured one.

### Is the named receiver real or decorative?

The primary receiver (`standards.digital.gov`) is a real, cited, in-scope target with a genuine unmeasured duty in its draft text — a fair fit on paper. But its practical usability by that body, *as currently designed*, is weak: no standards body would take an operational compliance number from an 11-URL sample using a whole-page diff with known chrome-contamination risk and a date-extractor whose SELF/OTHER labelling this house itself killed a session ago. The fit is rhetorically exact and operationally premature — this is a decorative-primary-receiver risk at increment-1 scale, not yet earned.

The secondary receiver (EDGI's web-monitoring project) is worse: the concept honestly discloses that "the direct route to that code host returned HTTP 403... its current liveness is reported, not confirmed by us." Naming an unconfirmed-live project as a receiver, however honestly caveated, is close to naming no receiver at all for that leg.

### Is this an arc or a one-night work in four increments?

§5's machine-advantage argument ("a single URL-month costs one archive fetch, and a population worth a per-authority profile is thousands of them") is the right argument *in principle*, but increment 1 itself — 11 URLs × 12 months = 132 fetches, throttled at 1.5s — is comfortably inside what one diligent analyst could run over an afternoon with the same public CDX API. Nothing about *this* increment is beyond hand-scale; the argument for machine-necessity rests entirely on increments 2–4 reaching "thousands," which is asserted, not currently backed by a stated plan for how the corpus scales past the existing 177-URL source pool. The arc's legitimacy is a forward promise, not yet a demonstrated fact — acceptable at a concept gate, but worth naming plainly rather than treating §5 as already discharged.

## (b) THE HOSTILE-CRITIC CHALLENGE — non-blocking, published verbatim

So a page said "Updated 27 June 2024" and sometimes that was a lie. Congratulations. Every web developer, every SEO consultant, every archivist who has ever looked sideways at a CMS template already assumes this. The concept's own footnote admits the plainest, most citable version of this claim already exists in the wild from a major crawler operator — and then declines to cite it, not because it's wrong, but because of a naming rule. That is not intellectual rigor, that is a footnote doing the work of a finding while the finding itself gets dressed up in four sessions of apparatus.

Look at what the actual instrument is: eleven URLs. Eleven. Split three, three, three, two across four "authorities," where the IE arm is two pages — two — chosen not because they're representative of Ireland's government web but because they're the only two out of fifty-six that happened to print a date in a prior run. A "per-authority profile" built on n=2 is not a profile, it's an anecdote wearing a lab coat. And the prediction that's supposed to carry the whole headline — "V fails to move... on at least one authority" — is worded so that it can be satisfied by that anecdote alone, with a sample small enough that noise does the work reality was supposed to do.

Then there's the tell that should worry anyone reading closely: this exact same team, two days ago, on largely the same pages, ran a blind human check on whether the dates their own script extracts are even about the page they're printed on — and found that outside one single authority (EC), the machine could not confirm a single one was. Not "some were wrong." Zero were confirmed right. And the next move is not to fix the extractor, or drop the contaminated authorities, or even flag it as a named risk in the "what would kill this" list — it's to reuse it "unmodified" and add a disclaimer sentence that says, in effect, *we're not claiming this measures what it looks like it measures.* That sentence is doing an enormous amount of load-bearing work for something that will ship as a chart with axis labels reading "authority" and a headline number under it. Nobody outside this house is going to read the disclaimer before they read the number.

And the whole apparatus — a concept gate, a pre-registration with locked predictions, a named receiver, a "nearest neighbours" literature review — sits on top of a method that hasn't solved the problem the house's *own prior work* already proved is the hard part of this exact question: telling apart "the page changed" from "the navbar rotated," and telling apart "this date is about the page" from "this date is about a citation on the page." Four increments of scaffolding around a diff threshold picked in advance and a regex-based date-grabber that's already been shown to grab the wrong date most of the time outside one authority. That's not scale, that's not repetition doing anything a person couldn't — it's a small, noisy sample dressed in the language of a per-authority instrument, and the paperwork is more rigorous than the pipe it's attached to.

Is it slop? Not fabricated — everything is sourced, the drops are disclosed, the ratios will ship in full. But "not fabricated" is a low bar. The honest version of this pitch is: *"we're going to run a diff on eleven government pages a month apart and see if the printed date agrees, using an extractor we already proved doesn't reliably know what it's reading."* That's a worthwhile afternoon's script. Whether it deserves a four-increment arc, a named federal-standards receiver, and a concept-gate ceremony is the real question this gate should be asking, and right now the answer leans toward: the ceremony is bigger than the finding it's built to carry.
