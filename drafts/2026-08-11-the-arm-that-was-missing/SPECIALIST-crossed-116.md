## 1. Is the estimator right?

**Verified independently, and correct.** I did not trust `crossed_model.py`'s own cross-checks; I wrote a brute-force `O(N²)` double loop (6.37 million pairs) over the actual day-3 rows, computing `(1/N²) Σ_{i,j} u_i u_j · 1[same account OR same page]` directly with no group-mean shortcuts:

```
brute force: v_direct = 5.7345354591801845e-05  ->  DEFF = 1.916066649353206
crossed_model.py reported:                            DEFF = 1.9160666493530465
difference: 1.59e-13   (floating-point noise)
```

I also recomputed the three one-way DEFFs (account, page, cell) from scratch and confirmed the inclusion–exclusion identity by hand-arithmetic on the numbers themselves, not on the code's own assertion:

```
account 1.4216183504243052 + page 1.8115115282044930 − cell 1.3170632292757232 = 1.916066649353075
```

This matches route 1 to 13 decimal places. So **yes, `DEFF_crossed = DEFF_account + DEFF_page − DEFF_cell` is exact**, and it is exact for a structural reason, not an empirical one: I traced the algebra by hand and confirmed that `sigma2_AP` in route 1 is *defined* as `T_C/M_C − sigma2_A − sigma2_P` (a residual), which forces `sigma2_A·M_A + sigma2_P·M_P + sigma2_AP·M_C ≡ T_A + T_P − T_C` for every dataset, by construction — not because the additive model happens to fit. Route 2 (CGM) is `V_A + V_P − V_AxP`, a linear combination of the same three one-way variances that DEFF_account/page/cell come from, so `DEFF_2way = DEFF_A + DEFF_P − DEFF_C` trivially (division by a shared `v_binom` is linear). Route 3 is the same double sum I computed by brute force. **The document's claim that all three routes are algebraically the same estimator is correct**, and the "agreement to 4.4×10⁻¹⁶" is not corroboration of anything.

One thing the document did *not* catch, which I found by re-reading its own pre-registration: **kill criterion K5** ("routes 1 and 2 differ by > 0.50 absolute DEFF → they are not measuring the same thing") is exactly as vacuous as P5, for the identical reason — it cannot fire given the algebra above. The scoring table in §7 records "K5 does not [fire]" as if it were informative; it isn't, and this should have been retracted alongside P5.

Is inclusion–exclusion **the right object for the variance of a simple mean**? Yes, conditionally: it is the exact variance of `ȳ` under the assumption that all pairwise dependence is fully explained by sharing an account or a page — i.e., that the covariance between two units sharing *neither* key is exactly zero. That is a real, testable-in-principle assumption, not something "model-free" in an absolute sense; the document's "does not depend on the additive model being true" is correct about the *additive combination* of the two effects, but the "model-free" framing glosses over the fact that the two-key exhaustiveness assumption is still an assumption. I found no test in the document of whether dependence exists outside these two keys (e.g., shared upload day, shared hosting domain, a platform-wide enforcement sweep) — see also §4 below.

I also verified numerically that the `K/(K−1)` finite-cluster factor breaks the exact identity, exactly as the pre-registration warns: with `fpc=True`, route 1 stays at 1.9160666 (unaffected — it doesn't use the factor per-route) while route 2 (CGM) moves to 1.9168334 — a 0.0008 gap. This is small, but worth flagging precisely because `addendum_116.py` uses the **fpc-applied** route-2 value (1.9899863) to correct all 36 intervals, not the no-fpc value (1.9891942) that the exact identity is proved on. The practical effect is negligible (`sqrt(1.98999/1.98919) = 1.0002`), but it is a real, if trivial, inconsistency between the algebra the document proves and the number it actually applies.

## 2. The negative variance component

The document treats `sigma2_AP = −0.03991248` as descriptive but essentially *real* — "the two effects are substitutes at the cell level, not additive" — while asserting the design effect is unaffected. Both halves need separating.

**The DEFF-is-unaffected claim is correct**, and I can show why precisely: `v_direct` (route 3) never references `sigma2_AP` at all — it uses the raw cross-product sum `SC` over same-cell pairs directly, which is a covariance and has no sign constraint. `sigma2_AP` only exists as an artifact of route 1's decomposition into named "variance components"; it plays no role in the number that gets published. This is correct and I verified it by inspecting the code: `v_model` uses `s2_C * M_C`, and I confirmed algebraically (§1) that this equals `T_C` regardless of whether `s2_C` "means" anything as a variance.

**The negative-component-as-real-substitution reading is overstated**, however. Two things the document had and did not report:

- `crossed-116.json`'s own component bootstrap computes a 95% interval for `sigma2_AP`: **[−0.1351, 0.0072]** — this *includes zero*. The document's table shows an em-dash for this row ("—") where the interval was actually computed and sits in the file. A confident causal reading ("substitutes, not additive") is not well supported by an interval that cannot rule out zero.
- The standard treatment of out-of-range moment-based variance component estimates, going back to Nelder (1954, cited in the 2024 paper below) and formalized by Thompson (1962), is that they most commonly indicate **sampling noise around a true value at or near the boundary**, not a "real" negative effect — variance-components-by-moments estimators are *unbiased but not constrained to the parameter space*, and a small-magnitude negative draw is the expected behavior when the true component is small and the number of qualifying pairs is limited (here `M_C` = 4,246 same-cell pairs, the smallest of the three pair classes). Thompson, W.A. Jr. (1962), "The Problem of Negative Estimates of Variance Components," *Ann. Math. Statist.* 33(1):273–289, https://projecteuclid.org/euclid.aoms/1177704731. A 2024 *Statistics in Medicine* paper I pulled the actual text from (via a hand-rolled PDF extractor, since standard tooling failed on this PDF) states the point plainly: *"the number of individuals (or data clusters) from whom random effects are to be estimated is small and a variance component has a small positive underlying value, sampling variability may result in a negative estimate ... [these] unlucky negative estimates ... are not the focus of this paper"* — i.e. that reading is treated in the literature as the *default*, not the exceptional, explanation. Bridge et al. (2024), DOI 10.1002/sim.10070, open-access PDF: https://researchonline.lshtm.ac.uk/id/eprint/4672610/13/Bridge-etal-2024-Negative-variance-components-and-intercept-slope-correlations-greater-than-one-in-magnitude.pdf.
- The standard *treatments* in that literature are: (a) report as-is (preserves unbiasedness of downstream linear combinations, which is what this document effectively does for the DEFF, correctly); (b) truncate at zero for reporting (introduces bias, and is explicitly what the document declines to do); (c) fit by REML instead of method-of-moments, which cannot go negative by construction but requires numerical optimization this arc's pure-Python constraint makes awkward (see Q5).

A related but distinct caveat also worth separating out: the CGM paper the document cites for route 2 explicitly discusses its **own** version of this problem — I extracted the actual text from the PDF myself (WebFetch could not parse it; I wrote a small zlib/regex extractor against the raw PDF streams) and confirmed the passage exists: *"the resulting variance estimate V[β] may have negative elements on the diagonal. In some applications with fixed effects, V[β] may be nonpositive-definite... [we use] the eigendecomposition of the estimated variance matrix and convert[] any negative eigenvalue(s) to zero"* (Cameron, Gelbach & Miller, https://cameron.econ.ucdavis.edu/research/JBESpaper2009version.pdf; published as *Robust Inference With Multiway Clustering*, JBES 29(2):238–249, 2011, DOI 10.1198/jbes.2010.07136). This is what kill criterion K2 checks for (`V_2way <= 0`), and it correctly does not fire here (`V_2way = 5.73×10⁻⁵ > 0`). But this is a **different** phenomenon from the negative `sigma2_AP` — CGM's caveat concerns the overall two-way variance estimate going negative; the document's negative component is a classical ANOVA-style variance-component artifact inside route 1's decomposition. The document's prose runs these two literatures together without distinguishing them; they have different standard remedies.

## 3. Is the correction applied correctly?

**(a) First-order `n_eff = n/DEFF` Wilson correction — a recognized method, with a documented failure mode that applies here.** I confirmed `power_audit.wilson()` implements the standard Wilson score interval correctly (matches the textbook closed form). Substituting an effective sample size into a Wilson-type interval is a real, published technique: I extracted text from a U.S. Census Bureau methodology paper (Franco, Little, Louis & Slud, "Comparative Study of Confidence Intervals for Proportions in Complex Sample Surveys," [link removed 2026-08-19 — a course page hosting another author's text; rights unsettled]) which explicitly studies "seven alternatives to the Wald interval for a binomial proportion with sample size replaced by the 'effective sample size'" and recommends Wilson-type intervals specifically. But the same paper's central finding is a direct caution against this addendum's method: *"For intervals that perform well in the SRS context, our results suggest that the principal cause of undercoverage in complex surveys is uncertainty in estimating the effective sample size,"* and *"all intervals ... under-cover when there is clustering and design effects are computed from a simple design-based estimator of sampling variance."* `addendum_116.py` plugs in a single point estimate of DEFF with no propagation of its own uncertainty (the bootstrap CI for the crossed DEFF, [1.35, 2.78], is wide — its own 95% interval nearly doubles — and none of that uncertainty reaches the 36 recomputed intervals). That is the textbook mechanism for undercoverage, present here and unaddressed.

**(b) Applying one pooled DEFF to 36 differently-composed populations — where it concretely breaks.** I pulled the actual `addendum-116.json` rows: they include per-age-band, per-stratum, per-cohort-year, and per-decoded-year cells, several with `n` as small as 1, 2, or 3. Two rows in particular expose the problem directly: **`INCREMENT-3 §1b — stratum W-article`** and **`stratum F-forum`** both receive the same pooled crossed DEFF (1.9900), but `gap_116.py`'s own arm-specific computation (which I re-ran and reproduced bit-for-bit) shows the *true* crossed design effect on those two strata is **2.3515** (article) and **1.3333** (forum)  — a factor of 1.76 apart. Quantified: the article-stratum interval, corrected at the pooled 1.99 instead of its own 2.35, is too narrow by a factor of `sqrt(2.3515/1.99) ≈ 1.086` (about 9%); the forum-stratum interval, corrected at 1.99 instead of its own 1.33, is *over*-widened by `sqrt(1.99/1.3333) ≈ 1.22` (about 22%). The document's own caveat ("For rows whose population is not that subset this is an approximation, and it is labelled one") is honest but not quantified — this is the quantification, and it shows the approximation is not small for exactly the two rows (article/forum) where the arc had already measured the true per-stratum figure and could have used it instead. It breaks worst at the extremes: `n_eff = n/DEFF` for the `n=1` ("creation year 1971") and `n=2` ("cohort 2018") cells comes out to 0.50 and 1.00 respectively — a design-effect correction is being applied to cells too small to contain even one effective independent observation, which is closer to a formal continuation of the formula than a meaningful correction. A general, source-backed version of the same point: design effects are "specific to a particular estimator" and vary across domains within a survey, so using one pooled value across heterogeneous subgroups is a known simplification (CRAN PracTools vignette, https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/PracTools/vignettes/Design-effects.html).

**(c) The gap and MH-OR inflations — algebraically sound, transportability unverified for one of the two.** Both formulas are internally correct: `Var(p_e − p_f) = Var(p_e)+Var(p_f)`, each term correctly scaled by its own arm's DEFF (the gap function even does this per-arm, more carefully than the addendum does), and for the MH odds ratio, inverting the published CI to recover an implied `se_log`, then multiplying by `sqrt(DEFF)`, is dimensionally correct algebra *given* the assumption `Var_complex(stat) = DEFF · Var_srs(stat)` transfers unchanged from a simple proportion to the compound statistic. That transportability assumption is exactly the same one flagged in (b), and I tested it directly for the two-proportion gap (see §5) — it held up well there. I did **not** find, and the document does not report, any equivalent direct check for the Mantel–Haenszel log-odds-ratio; the `sqrt(DEFF)` scaling there is unverified extrapolation, not a demonstrated result. That is a specific, nameable gap, not a general one.

## 4. Is the bootstrap valid?

The resampling scheme (draw components with replacement, recompute every statistic from linear per-component aggregates) is **exactly implemented** — I reproduced `crossed_model.py`'s full run bit-for-bit (`python3 crossed_model.py ledger/run-2026-08-13T0427Z.json` regenerated `crossed-116.json` byte-identically), so the "linear in per-component aggregates, exact not approximate" claim is not just asserted, it is verifiably true of this code.

Whether **components are a valid independence unit** is a different question, and the answer is: valid *only relative to the two-key model*, and I found concrete evidence the resulting inference is less robust than presented. Under the additive model, covariance between two units is nonzero only if they directly share an account or a page — the model assigns exactly zero covariance to units in the same connected component but not directly linked (e.g., linked only through an intermediate account or page). So "every dependence this model can express lies inside a connected component" is true by construction, but that is circular as a validation: it tests the model's own boundary, not whether real-world dependence respects that boundary. What it **misses**: any dependence not carried by the account/page keys at all — a platform-wide enforcement action on a given day, shared hosting/CDN infrastructure, topical/regional correlation, or timing of the crawl — would induce correlation *between* components that this resampling scheme cannot see or correct for, however many components there are. That is conjecture about the mechanism (I have no data to test it against), but it follows directly from reading what the model can and cannot represent, and the document's own §9 disclaimer ("nothing here is a measurement of the platform") gestures at this without naming it as a limit on the bootstrap specifically.

More concretely, I built an **independent, non-random delete-one-component jackknife** on the identical day-3 component structure (my own code, not a rerun of theirs, no shared seed) and it does not corroborate the headline "P2 holds" framing as cleanly as presented:

```
full-sample sigma2_P:                0.04019099620813836
arc's percentile bootstrap 95% CI:   [0.00218773, 0.13390395]   (excludes 0, barely)
my delete-one-component jackknife:   [-0.03951606, 0.11989806]  (includes 0)

arc's percentile bootstrap 95% CI, DEFF_crossed: [1.34823, 2.78388]
my jackknife 95% CI, DEFF_crossed:               [1.06487, 2.76727]
```
Full script and output are in `/tmp/.../scratchpad/jackknife_check.py`. The percentile bootstrap's own lower bound for `sigma2_P` (0.00219) sits extremely close to zero — the document reports this as a clean pass ("P2 holds") without noting how boundary-adjacent it is, and a standard alternative variance estimate on the same component partition does not reproduce the exclusion. This is consistent with, and reinforces, the document's own §5 finding that 84% of `sigma2_P` is attributable to a single heavy page: a statistic that concentrated in one or a few components is exactly where percentile-bootstrap and jackknife methods are expected to disagree, and where "the interval excludes zero" is a fragile way to state the finding.

## 5. Strongest alternative analysis deliverable in pure Python

The most valuable thing not done, and the most directly buildable given the constraint (no numpy/scipy), is to **stop borrowing a proportion-level design effect and scaling a normal-approximation SE by `sqrt(DEFF)`, and instead bootstrap the derived statistic itself at the component level** — the machinery for this already exists in `crossed_model.py` (the `components()` / `comp_aggregates()` / resample-with-replacement loop), it just needs to carry per-component, per-stratum 2×2 tables instead of only the account/page aggregates it currently tracks.

I built and ran this for the encyclopedia-vs-forum gap (`/tmp/.../scratchpad/component_bootstrap_gap.py`, independent code, independent seed, 10,000 draws, day-2 population):

```
components: 2402, units: 3575, components spanning BOTH strata: 6 of 2402
gap point estimate: 3.7398 pp
direct component-bootstrap 95% CI:  [-0.4559, 7.9762]   (excludes 0: False)
gap_116.py's sqrt(DEFF)-scaled CI:  [-0.3317, 8.2527]   (excludes 0: False)
```
The two intervals agree closely (within ~0.3 pp at both ends, same conclusion). This is a genuine, useful result: it is evidence, not assumption, that the `sqrt(DEFF)` shortcut was not badly wrong for the gap statistic in this instance — something the original document asserted without checking. The same result also shows the two strata are almost entirely graph-disjoint (6 of 2402 components touch both), which is worth knowing on its own.

**What should be done with the same, already-built machinery, and was not:** extend `comp_aggregates` to also carry the per-stratum 2×2 counts feeding the Mantel–Haenszel odds ratio, and bootstrap the MH statistic directly across component resamples rather than inflating its published SE by `sqrt(DEFF)`. Nothing about this needs numerical libraries — MH weights are closed-form arithmetic over cell counts, exactly the kind of linear-in-aggregates quantity the existing bootstrap loop is built for. This is the concrete next step I would prioritize, because it is the one derived-statistic correction in this session that was never checked against anything, unlike the gap (checked here) or the proportions (checked by construction via the Wilson formula itself).

A second, cheap, and complementary addition: the delete-one-component jackknife I used in §4 is deterministic, needs no seed, costs `O(components)` given the existing total-minus-one aggregate trick, and should be run alongside every percentile-bootstrap CI in this arc as a standard cross-check — it would have caught the `sigma2_P` fragility before publication rather than after.

A third, more structural option for the negative-variance-component problem in §2: a **boundary-respecting (non-negative-constrained) refit** of the three variance components — e.g., a simple coordinate-search or grid search in pure Python that finds the closest non-negative `(sigma2_A, sigma2_P, sigma2_AP)` triple to the unconstrained method-of-moments solution (a discrete analogue of Thompson's 1962 approach, or Henderson's Method III with truncation) — would show directly whether forcing `sigma2_AP ≥ 0` moves the crossed DEFF at all. Given that `v_direct` doesn't use `sigma2_AP`'s sign (§1–2), I'd predict this changes little, but that prediction should be demonstrated, not assumed, and it is buildable without numerical libraries.

## What the document overstates or understates

- **Overstates**: the causal reading of the negative interaction component ("the two effects are substitutes... not additive") is asserted more confidently than its own (computed, unreported) bootstrap interval supports — that interval includes zero.
- **Overstates**: "P2 holds" is presented as a clean pass; an independent jackknife on the same component partition gives a `sigma2_P` interval that includes zero, and the bootstrap's own lower bound (0.0022) is very close to the boundary. This should have been flagged as fragile rather than settled.
- **Understates**: the magnitude of the pooled-DEFF-on-heterogeneous-subpopulations problem in §4/addendum — the arc had already measured (in the same session) that the article and forum strata have crossed design effects of 2.35 and 1.33 respectively, nearly a factor of 2 apart, yet applied the same 1.99 to both in the 36-interval addendum without quantifying the resulting error (roughly 9% too narrow for article, roughly 22% too wide for forum).
- **Understates**: the parallel defect in kill criterion K5, which is exactly as vacuous as prediction P5 for the same algebraic reason, and was not retracted alongside it.
- **Understates**: "model-free" for route 3 is model-free only with respect to additive combination of the two effects, not with respect to the assumption that the two clustering keys exhaust all dependence; no test of dependence beyond account/page is attempted or claimed.
- **Correctly stated, and independently reproduced by me**: the three-routes identity, the inclusion–exclusion decomposition, the P3 (heaviest-page) finding, the P7 day-2/day-3 stability finding, the 100% attribution (K3), and all headline numbers in `crossed-116.json`, `crossed-116-day2.json`, `addendum-116.json`, `gap-116.json`, and `derived-116.json` — all four scripts reproduce their own committed JSON outputs bit-for-bit when rerun, and I independently re-derived the core DEFF numbers by brute force from the raw ledger rather than trusting the arc's own functions.
