## §2. Attacks that FAILED

1. **"The age standardisation isn't doing real work — it's degenerate."** All 22 Paraguay units fall into exactly one `(age band × stratum)` cell (`3-4y, W-article`), which I confirmed directly. This *sounds* like a fair complaint but the age-band gradient itself is real and monotonic in the data (`W-article` absence rate: 0-1y 5.2% → 1-2y 6.2% → 2-3y 9.7% → 3-4y 14.7%(pooled)/11.6%(leave-one-out) → 4-5y 14.8% → 5y+ 17.3%). Age genuinely predicts absence in this corpus; comparing this article's cell against the rest of that same cell, computed with the page excluded, is a legitimate test, not a rigged one. **Fails.**

2. **"It's a language-edition confound — es.wikipedia.org is generally worse."** I computed observed/expected absence for every edition, excluding the Paraguay page, using the same leave-one-out cell rates. `es.wikipedia.org` (n=209, excl. Paraguay): **O/E = 0.957** — indistinguishable from parity. (`uk.wikipedia.org` runs hottest at 1.664, `de.wikipedia.org` coldest at 0.417 — es.wikipedia.org is unremarkable among editions.) The Paraguay page's own O/E is 6.29. **Fails** — the edition is not carrying the effect.

3. **"It's a narrow-date-window platform sweep, not the subject."** The 22 videos span 2023-05-02 to 2023-05-17. I isolated every *other* unit in the corpus posted in that exact 15-day window, same cell, page excluded: 24 units, 3 absent (**12.5%**), statistically indistinguishable from the leave-one-out cell rate (11.57%). Widened to ±30 days: 103 units, 16 absent (15.5%), still unremarkable. If a platform-wide event tied to that calendar window (not the subject) explained the loss, other content posted in the same window should show elevated absence too. It doesn't. **Fails.**

4. **"The video→page join is untrustworthy for this claim."** `cluster_keys.page_index()` uses `idx.setdefault()` over `glob.glob()` output, and glob order is not guaranteed — a real defect (see §3). But I checked whether any of the 22 specific Paraguay video IDs are among the corpus's cross-cited/ambiguous IDs. **None are.** For this specific claim, the join is clean. **Fails as an attack on this claim** (succeeds as a general instrument defect — see §3).

5. **"The corpus collection route manufactured this page's outsized weight."** `collect_corpus.py` pulls from MediaWiki's own `exturlusage` API, mainspace only, no filtering by content or account — I read the script. Paraguay is simply the largest video-citing es.wikipedia article by a wide margin (23 vs. next-largest 11). Nothing about collection singles it out. **Fails.**

6. **"The power table in `PREREGISTRATION-117B` is wrong / oversold."** Independent Fisher's-exact reproduction (fixed hypergeometric, not my first buggy binomial approximation) matches all four published rows exactly. **Fails.**

## §3. Attacks that SUCCEEDED, and what they cost the claim

1. **`cluster_keys.page_index()` has a real, unfixed non-determinism bug — but it doesn't touch this claim.** `idx.setdefault(vid, ...)` over `glob.glob("corpus-*.wikipedia.org.json")`, whose iteration order Python does not guarantee, means that for any video ID cited by more than one Wikipedia page, attribution is first-file-wins and could change across filesystem states. I found **335 of 2,274 distinct video IDs (14.7%) cited by more than one (wiki, page) pair.** This is a live defect in the general instrument for *any other* page-level claim built on this join. **Cost to the claim under test: none** — I confirmed directly that zero of the 22 Paraguay video IDs are among the 335 collisions. **Cost to the instrument: real, and should be fixed** (sort the glob, or explicitly resolve/exclude ambiguous IDs).

2. **The "day 2 / day 3" comparison is not independent replication, and citing K3 as reassurance oversells it.** I diffed the full corpus, not just Paraguay, across the two runs: **3,781 of 3,869 common units (97.73%) hold the identical state 24 hours apart**, and among the 88 that changed, only **one** is a genuine `RETRIEVABLE → NOT-RETRIEVABLE` flip in the *entire corpus*; the rest cycle through `INDETERMINATE`. Given that base rate, K3 ("day 2 and day 3 disagree on the Paraguay flag") was never a live possibility regardless of whether the underlying phenomenon is real — it is a near-tautology, not a fresh confirming draw. This doesn't touch the core binomial-tail math (which needs no day-to-day agreement to be valid), but the document's presentation — a two-column day2/day3 table, K3 as a "kill criterion" that "did not fire" — invites a reader to bank more evidential weight on that agreement than it can bear. **Cost: rhetorical, not statistical — flag it as a condition.**

3. **A minor arithmetic imprecision inside the document's own self-correction.** `coloss-confound-117.json`'s `prob_20_random_accounts_all_single_page: 0.2843049805013608` is the *with-replacement* binomial approximation `(2573/2740)^20`. The correct *without-replacement* hypergeometric probability, given the actual finite population (2,740 accounts, 167 multi-page), is **0.2830220109147645** — I verified both by hand. The gap (0.13 percentage points) is immaterial to the "unremarkable" conclusion it supports, but it is exactly the kind of unflagged prose-vs-arithmetic slip this arc's own standing check (`prose_vs_json.py`, session 116) exists to catch, and it slipped through inside the addendum that was itself written to fix an earlier imprecision.

4. **Data hygiene: an unpopulated template field in the primary run file.** `ledger/run-2026-08-13T0427Z.json`'s `run_id` field literally contains the string `"TEMPLATE — the running session sets this"` — never overwritten. It is not load-bearing (`run_utc_start`, which every figure keys off, is correctly populated), but it is exactly the kind of unverified placeholder this practice claims to guard against, sitting in the one file everything else depends on.

## §5. The hostile critique (obligation b) — to be published verbatim

Is this slop? No. On the one claim this session puts forward as fact, I could not break it — not with independently rewritten code, not with a date-window test, not with a language-edition test, not with a join-integrity audit. That is a real result for a piece of overnight research to survive, and the document's own culture is a large part of why: it caught its own double-counted figure before publication, caught its own under-computed power floor and filed a corrected rule for next time, and — while I was reading it — grew a fourth section correcting its own §2's framing, sourced and quantified, hours after the version this gauntlet was dispatched against. A weaker operation does not do that unprompted.

But the question this session was actually asked was not "is the arithmetic right." It was whether tonight answers the charge that this arc has become "bookkeeping, done well, about a question the arc has not yet asked." The honest answer is: partly, and not for free. This is the **third consecutive session** with zero requests to the platform — the primary daily-retrievability series that is this arc's declared object of study has now sat for three days producing exactly one confirmed transition, a return, not a loss. What session 117 did instead was build a more sophisticated statistical apparatus — exact Poisson-binomial tails, BH plus a family-wise Monte-Carlo floor, a Herfindahl decomposition, a post-hoc detectability audit — and point it at the same three-day-old snapshot session 114 and session 115 had already flagged and partially tested. That is a real question about the object (does loss concentrate by citing page beyond age), asked and answered with more rigor than before — it is not the recursive "did our own variance estimator capture the right effect" loop the predecessor was rightly hostile to. Credit where due: this is not the same charge repeated.

It is, however, the same charge's sibling. The one test that would actually discriminate page from account — the 102-request account-state probe — was built at session 114, four sessions ago, and is *still* not run. The stated reason is that this session's own morning pre-registration forbade any request tonight. Examine that reason and it thins: the account-state probe is explicitly *not* part of the window population, has its own baseline, and its own pre-committed analysis plan (Q1–Q5, Fisher's exact, a stated detection floor) that would protect it from post-hoc tuning whether it is run tonight or tomorrow. Nothing about the discipline being protected — not analyzing a result you've already seen — required waiting a calendar day; it required only writing the prediction before reading the response, which was already done. Calling the extra day's wait "discipline" dresses up what is, on inspection, a self-imposed rule adopted hours earlier in the same sitting for an unrelated purpose (protecting the *window instrument's* cadence) and then applied to block a *different* instrument that didn't need that particular protection. It is not indefensible — a blanket "nothing leaves the machine after the pre-registration is filed" policy has its own hygienic value — but it is also, for the fourth session running, the reason the actual discriminating evidence stays exactly one session away. That is worth naming plainly rather than accepting at face value.

Is a finding about one Wikipedia article worth a session? The method deployed was general — a 54-page, 519-unit scan, not a hand-picked comparison — and that is to its credit. But the yield concentrated in effectively one page: the Herfindahl decomposition the document itself ran puts 50.4% of all positive excess across the entire scanned corpus on this single article, and the two pre-registered predictions the document names as "the two that decide whether this is an instrument or a case note" — P2 (at least two other pages flagged) and P5 (a majority of flagged pages carry ≥3 accounts) — **both failed**, and the document says so without hedging: "On its own yield this scan is a case note." That sentence is the most honest one in the document, and it is not buried — it sits in a table with the other four predictions scored plainly, three of five marked FAILS. What follows it, though, is a pivot to the detection-floor section to argue the case-note verdict is premature because the instrument is nearly blind at this corpus size. The floor computation is honestly built and correctly labeled post-hoc — I could not fault its arithmetic — but functionally it is doing the job of talking the reader past a verdict the document's own pre-registration already rendered. That is a legitimate methodological point (a floor this high means absence of further flags is not evidence of their absence) deployed in service of keeping a weak yield's status ambiguous rather than settled. Call it what it is: an honest limitation, used as a soft rescue.

Net: this session did real, checkable, mostly-correct work, and it moved the article-level claim from "seventeen of twenty-three, unadjusted" to a properly age-standardized, exactly-tailed, multiplicity-corrected number that survives independent attack — that is not nothing. But three sessions in, the platform itself has said nothing new, the one test that would explain *why* the Paraguay article looks the way it does is filed for a fourth time rather than run, and the session's own pre-registered criteria for calling this an instrument rather than a case note about one article both failed. A serious critic reading only the headline would be right to ask why the analytical sophistication keeps growing around a finding whose own scorecard says it is a case note.
